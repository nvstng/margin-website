If one is managing one's own or other people's money, this is a really important thing to ponder on. Am I a good investor? Are my decisions going to lead me to making a meaningful alpha over, well, let's say what a conservative index fund will give?

Let's take the example of a growth at reasonable prices (GARP) investor. Such an investor invests in high growth companies which are also available at reasonable prices. In short, the investor seeks mispricing in businesses that have strong growth prospects, so that one doesn't get stuck in neglected stocks for long periods due to low growth.

If the investor is sound in process, psychology and effort, then one can make good returns, let's say around 20% XIRR over a long period. Now how does the investor know that he or she is indeed a good investor? What is one's evidence for it?

One may say, why not measure your XIRR and let the data speak for itself? But to decide based on this, I think it will be a mistake for at least the first 8 to 10 years. Wow! Yes, hear me out.

Let's take an investor who indeed ends up making 20% annualised return over 20 years. The look-back XIRR for that investor is likely to look like the diagram below, where the x-axis is the number of years in the journey and the y-axis is the look-back XIRR. Look-back XIRR is simply:

![Look-back XIRR for a 20% investor, converging over 20 years](/assets/images/good-investor-xirr-dispersion.png)

*This is a simplified chart. It is likely more brutal than this.*

Now let's see what is happening psychologically to this solid investor. He or she is being taken through a roller coaster ride in the initial years.

- Year 3, 28%: I am superhuman
- Year 6, 13%: Hmmm, big deal

Notice two things. First, the early years are extremely noisy and can oscillate between euphoria and disappointment. Second, as time passes, the dispersion reduces and the look-back XIRR starts stabilizing around the investor's underlying skill level.

It is only in year 8 to 10 that the person starts to get the feedback that he or she is upto something here, as one is consistently above 15% and tending slightly upwards.

---

Now let's take another investor who is not as good, but holds the same conviction in his or her method as the previous one. This investor never makes it. In other words, even in the long run this investor tends towards below average returns. The difficult truth is that for many years, one may simply not know whether one possesses skill or merely confidence. Conviction is necessary but insufficient. Long-term realized outcomes are ultimately the strongest evidence available.

A couple of things.

## Why does the chart dissipate with time?

This is simply because as the weight of time increases, a 20 to 30% swing in portfolio value doesn't have as much impact on till-date XIRR. For example, 100 rupees after 15 years at 20% CAGR is 1540 rupees. A 30% drop means 1078 rupees, which is still around 17% CAGR.

## Why can't one get feedback by comparing to an index?

Firstly, one must not compare the long-term average of an index with their own annual returns. For an investor, what matters is what the index does when his or her clock starts. So let's discuss it by comparing against the plot of the index.

![Look-back XIRR of the investor versus the index, both converging](/assets/images/good-investor-vs-index.png)

Indexes like NIFTY 50 and NIFTY 250 give lower return but are also less volatile. So in the initial years one is going above and below the index. Since active portfolios are more volatile than diversified indices, short-horizon relative performance itself fluctuates heavily, delaying useful comparison.

---

This is a brutally long test of human conviction and expertise both. It is completely worth it because doing it right can be life changing, due to compounding for long periods. It is also not worth it if it is done wrong, as one can lose a lot compared to not doing anything actively and simply investing in a diversified index fund.
